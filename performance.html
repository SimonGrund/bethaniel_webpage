Two Bettys, two different models

Local Betty and Betty in the Cloud run the same pipeline — the same checks, the same reviewer — on different models. So this page measures them separately, on the same fixtures, the same day.

The cloud finds more — most of all the wrong-but-real words and the dropped words, which are the errors that need a model to understand the sentence — and raises fewer false alarms. Local Betty is a little more often right about the fix when she does find something, and she never sends a word of your manuscript anywhere. Choose on that: privacy and no cost per book, or the stronger read.

New: missing words

“They expect us to look each other in the same place.” A dropped word is invisible to a spell checker — every word that is there is spelled right — and it is the error an author reads straight past, because the mind supplies the word.

Betty now reads for them in a pass of its own: it looks only for sentences a small word has fallen out of, keeps only a suggestion that adds exactly one such word between two others, and asks whether the sentence parses with it and not without. Surfaced, by language:

It is also a check that has to earn its false alarms, so we read it against 34,800 words of real novels outside the benchmark as well: on modern prose it suggested a missing word where none was missing twice on Local Betty and never in the cloud. Nineteenth-century prose, whose constructions can read as a dropped word, drew more.

What happens when it is wrong

A single accuracy figure hides the thing you actually care about, which is what a wrong suggestion costs you.

1 in 8 · 1 in 12

Betty never applies anything on its own, so the failure that matters is the one you cannot see. An error it finds but mis-fixes still arrives in front of you with a bad suggestion attached — you reject it and fix it yourself. An error it misses is invisible. That is why we report surfaced first, and the correct fix as a share of what was surfaced.

The same goes for the suggestions that turn out wrong. Our benchmark records one correct answer per error; real prose usually allows several. Of 110 wrong suggestions from Local Betty and 82 from the cloud, 13 and 7 introduced a word no dictionary knows. The rest left you with correct text that simply differed from the answer we wrote down.

The commas

Three in ten, as we score them. We are not going to dress that up — but we will say what it measures.

Commas are scored as reconstructing the original text: every planted comma error has exactly one right answer, the comma in the text it was planted into. That is not a true measure of comma quality. Comma placement is the rule that varies most between careful writers, and between houses, so a pass that punctuates differently but defensibly loses points here twice — once for the comma it did not restore, and once, as a false alarm, for the one it placed instead.

Even so, it is the weakest result on this page, and it is not a size problem: the cloud's larger model finds no more of them than the model on your laptop. Betty is deliberately conservative here — it would rather leave your commas alone than reflow your sentences to a style you did not ask for. Plan a human pass on punctuation. If that is the part of copy editing you were hoping to hand over entirely, Betty is not there yet, and we would rather you knew before you bought.

About one kettle.

Copy-editing a 60,000-word novel with Local Betty used a little less electricity than it takes to boil a litre of water — we measured it.*

In the cloud we estimate about the same, at most two. The bigger saving is the hours: the same pass by hand is twenty to forty at a desk. You still read every suggestion — but in about one.

* Measured on a desktop graphics card (RTX 5090): 60 Wh for 60,000 words, about 80–90 Wh for the whole computer; a litre boiled is about 110 Wh. The cloud's servers cannot be metered, so its figure is an estimate: 0.5 to 1 million tokens, at published energy figures for models of its size. Translation runs on a far larger model in a data centre, and is not one kettle.

How this was measured

Short stories written natively in each of the five languages rather than translated, so the mistakes are the ones that language actually makes, plus one text per language with only dropped words planted in it. Errors are planted by a generator that refuses any edit it cannot prove creates exactly one error of exactly the intended kind — so the ground truth is constructed, not annotated afterwards, and cannot drift from the text. Each story also runs clean, and everything Betty suggests there is a false alarm.

Both engines ran the full pipeline on the same afternoon: the Speed preset, grammar checks active, the reviewer on. The cloud run used a real credential, issued through the same checkout an author uses (with a 100% discount code). Every run records the pipeline that produced it, and the benchmark refuses to start with its grammar checks disabled — both of those exist because a run without them once produced numbers eight to twenty points low that looked entirely normal, and we published them.

Reproduce it with scripts/test-models.ts against the fixtures in sample_texts/, then scripts/performance-summary.ts, which computes every figure on this page and says exactly how. The report names every missed error and every false alarm individually; that detail is where most of the fixes on this page came from.
