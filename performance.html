What Betty Actually Catches

Both bundled models and the paid cloud one, four languages, roughly a hundred errors planted at known offsets in each. Every number on this page comes from a benchmark anyone with the repository can re-run — including the ones that are not flattering. Betty is built to be read over your shoulder, not trusted blindly, and these numbers are written for someone who intends to review every suggestion.

How to read this

A copy editor is judged on two things that pull against each other. Recall is the share of real errors it found. Precision is the share of its suggestions that were real errors. Pushing either one up tends to push the other down, and a tool that flags everything scores brilliantly on the first and is useless.

There is a third number, and in practice it is the one to read first: flags on clean text. Each language's fixture comes in two versions — one with errors planted, one with none. What a model says about the clean one is what an author actually experiences as noise, because there is nothing there to find. A tool that misses errors is disappointing. A tool that invents them makes the author distrust every suggestion, including the good ones.

The fixtures

Four texts of about 2,270 words each — the same short story, written natively in each language rather than translated, so the errors are the ones that language actually makes. Roughly a hundred errors per language are then planted by a generator that refuses any edit which does not produce exactly one span of the intended kind. The ground truth is therefore constructed, not annotated after the fact, so it cannot drift from the text.

Every number here is reproducible rather than averaged. Corrections are decoded greedily, and the benchmark now runs one request at a time, which makes a run repeatable to the digit: four repeats of the same fixture came back identical, as did five runs with different random seeds. Earlier versions of this page averaged two repeats because several requests in flight at once were batched together by the inference engine, and the batching changed the arithmetic enough to move the same fixture between 50% and 71%. Numbers measured that way carried roughly twenty points of noise; these do not.

Spelling — where Betty is strongest

Betty fixes 88–96% of misspellings in English, Danish and Spanish outright, and flags almost all of the rest. Where it is not sure of the right spelling it still marks the word rather than guessing quietly, so the error reaches you either way.

German is the exception, and it is worth saying why. German builds new words by gluing existing ones together, so a great many perfectly correct German words are absent from any dictionary. That weakens the signal Betty leans on hardest, and German misspelling recall sits near half the others. If you write in German, treat spelling as assisted rather than covered.

Wrong words — all or nothing

A wrong word is two real words confused: then for than, Danish nogen for nogle, ad for af. No dictionary can catch these, because both spellings are correct. Only something that follows the sentence can.

This is the one place a miss is invisible. With a misspelling, a bad guess still leaves a flag you can act on. With a wrong word there is no flag at all — the sentence simply reads as though nothing is wrong. Danish is the weak case: roughly three of every four planted confusions passed through silently. Betty should not be your only check here, in any language.

Commas — help, not authority

Comma use varies from writer to writer, so Betty treats commas as suggestions rather than corrections. On these test texts it restores anywhere from 11% of the commas the original author had written (Spanish) to 70% (German) — the widest spread of any category on this page.

Commas are also the largest single category in every fixture — more planted commas than misspellings in three of the four languages — so this gap accounts for most of the errors Betty misses overall.

Betty will help you with commas. It will not finish them. Where the two of you disagree, assume it is your book: no program can capture a personal rhythm, and a comma you would not have written is not an error. But where commas matter to you, plan a human pass. This is the part of Betty we are least satisfied with, and we would rather you heard it from us than found it yourself.

What it costs you

The number that matters most is not how much Betty finds — it is how often it interrupts you for nothing. Each fixture also exists in a clean version with no errors at all. Anything Betty says about that version is pure noise.

Betty marks its own uncertainty. A suggestion it is confident in arrives plainly; one a second reviewer doubted arrives flagged for your judgement. Both columns below are on a clean chapter of roughly 2,270 words.

Expect about one confidently wrong suggestion per chapter — often none — and a handful more that Betty has already marked as uncertain. Those are worth a glance, not a fight. Keep your eyes open on the marked ones and you will spend very little time arguing with a machine.

On a chapter that does contain errors, Betty also averages around seven suggestions that point at nothing and a dozen that land on a real problem while proposing the wrong fix. The second kind still does its job: it shows you where to look.

English and German are where the paid cloud model earns its cost — it leads both, largely on commas, where it finds 59% in English against roughly a third for the bundled models.

Danish is the one to be careful about. The cloud model finds Danish misspellings better than anything else on this page, then contributes almost nothing else in that language: no commas at all, and the fewest wrong words. Baby Betty — the small model that ships with the app and runs on your own machine — is the better Danish editor.

If you write in Danish or Spanish, start with the bundled models. There is no version of this page in which paying more makes your Danish manuscript better.

Line edit

The line-edit and translation figures below come from an earlier version of this benchmark, before the reproducibility fix described above. They carry the run-to-run noise that the copy-edit numbers no longer do, and will be re-measured.

Line editing is scored the same way — planted style errors, recall and precision — and the numbers are much lower. Some of that is real and some of it is the measurement, and it is worth being clear about which.

A "false positive" in the clean column is a suggestion that no planted error asked for — which for a line edit is very often just a line edit. Asked to improve the rhythm of a paragraph that contains no mistakes, a good editor still has opinions. The planted-error frame measures whether the model found the specific clumsiness that was deliberately introduced; it says nothing about whether the other suggestions were good.

A reference-free quality metric that does answer that question — perplexity reduction gated by an edit-distance ceiling, in the shape of the published Scribendi Score — is implemented and unit-tested in the codebase, but is not yet wired into this benchmark. The line-edit numbers here are the planted-error scores, and should be read as a floor rather than a verdict.

Translation

Translation has something copy editing does not: a human reference to compare against. The source text is translated into each language, and the result scored by chrF — character n-gram overlap with a human translation of the same passage, the standard automatic metric for this — plus two checks chrF is blind to.

Both models land in the low-to-mid 70s, which for chrF is a serviceable translation rather than a publishable one. The larger model leads in all three target languages, though by very different amounts — nearly three points into German, two and a half into Danish, and barely half a point into Spanish.

The length and leak columns exist because chrF is easy to fool. A translation that stops early, or that leaves a proper noun and a handful of idioms in English, can still score well on character overlap; a reader notices both immediately. Length ratios here sit within a few percent of 1.00 and leakage under 2%, so in this case chrF is measuring what it looks like it is measuring.

The paragraph-level fluency reviewer that runs inside the real translation pass is scored separately, by a model judging translated paragraphs 1–5. It flagged six of ten paragraphs on the smaller model's Danish output — garbled words, an invented "his father" for "her father" — and none on the larger model's. That reviewer's job in production is to catch exactly those and re-translate them.

Betty in the Cloud

The paid cloud option runs a much larger model — Meta-Llama-3.3-70B on OVHcloud AI Endpoints — through the same pipeline, and it appears in every chart above rather than in a footnote.

One caveat belongs with every cloud number on this page: a cloud job is forced to the Speed preset, which uses one editor agent, because Bethaniel pays for those tokens. Both bundled models run two agents whose findings are pooled. So the cloud column is the configuration you actually get, not a like-for-like comparison of model sizes — the larger model is doing more with less of the pipeline.

Where the gap actually comes from

Half of it is the editor count. A cloud job is forced to the Speed preset — one editor agent — because Bethaniel pays for those tokens, while a local run defaults to two agents whose findings are pooled. Run the cloud model on two editors like the local ones and English recall goes from 41% to 48%. That is a deliberate cost decision, but it means the table above compares a one-editor run against two-editor runs.

The rest is span granularity, and it is the more interesting half. The same model was run through the same fixture in both places — Qwen3.5-9B, which is Big Bad Betty's own model, quantised locally and full-precision in the cloud. The local copy scored 60% recall. The cloud copy scored 35%, at identical precision.

Locally the models return surgical spans — hand and it becomes hand, and it. Through the cloud they return rewritten sentences instead, replacing a whole clause to fix one comma inside it. The benchmark credits a correction only where it lands on a planted error and fixes it the planted way, so a fifteen-word rewrite that repairs three planted commas is scored as one merged span and misses the other two. Low recall, unchanged precision — exactly the shape of the numbers.

This is not only a scoring artefact. A sentence-level rewrite is worse copy editing than a comma insertion: the author has to accept or reject the whole sentence rather than the one thing that was wrong. It is being fixed, and these numbers will move when it is.

Two contributing causes are known. The sampling settings the local models run with — a top-k cap and a repetition penalty — never reach an API model: they are read from a per-call override that nothing sets, so the configured values are silently dropped. OVHcloud rejects top_k outright in any case, so that half cannot be matched on this provider. Qwen models there also reason by default and will spend an entire output budget thinking and return nothing, which is why the earlier cloud measurement of that model was so poor.

An earlier number that did not reproduce

This page previously reported the cloud model at 56% recall on the English fixture, from a single run made while choosing which of five OVHcloud models to use. Two repeats of the identical fixture now measure 41% on the shipped one-editor configuration.

Fifteen points is far outside what the rest of this page treats as noise, so it is worth saying which parts moved. Spelling barely did, because a dictionary is deterministic. Every category the model itself drives did — wrong word 53% → 40%, dialect 89% → 67%, capitalisation 71% → 43%. The two runs also used different harnesses.

Take the honest reading: a single run of this model is not worth much. The numbers in the table are two repeats and belong to the same harness as every other number here, which is why they are the ones reported — but the run-to-run spread on the cloud model is visibly wider than on the local ones.

The 70B was still the right pick among the five cloud models tested — it led the next best by twenty-one points of recall and by a factor of twenty on clean-text false positives, at half the token price. That comparison was one run per model, and it now looks likely that every cloud candidate was measured through the same handicap.

The whole four-language run costs about EUR 0.30 in tokens and is a single command (scripts/test-models.ts --api), so these numbers are cheap to keep honest as the model changes.

What was fixed, and what it teaches

Four bugs found by this benchmark, listed because they share a shape worth recognising: a heuristic that is correct in English, silently wrong in one other language, and invisible until a large fixture exists in that language.

  • Spanish — the grammar server rewrote every quotation mark. One rule converted straight quotes to angled ones: 248 flags across the corpus, not one of them on a real error. Precision went from 31% to 67% and clean-text false positives from 71 to zero. One line of code.
  • Danish — a third of the dictionary was unreadable. 26,232 entries carried optional annotations the old spell checker did not strip, so den, havde and kom were all unknown words. Clean-text flags fell from 34 to 3.
  • German — the spell checker rejected 87,955 words from its own dictionary. Not a content gap. German capitalises every noun, so kommen collided with Kommen and the old checker kept only one of each pair. Danish loses one such pair in 1,240; English and Spanish lose none, which is why nobody had noticed.
  • German — every capitalised typo was protected as a name. The pipeline guards mid-sentence capitals as probable proper nouns, which is right in English, Danish and Spanish and describes every noun in German. It was discarding all 27 capitalised planted misspellings before the dictionary was ever consulted, even though the dictionary rejected all 44. Replacing the signal with recurrence — a character's name comes back, a typo does not — took German misspelling recall from 49% to 68% and overall German recall from 59% to 67%, with no change in any other language.

A related result, recorded because it saved work rather than created it: the dictionaries are all already current. Compared byte-for-byte against upstream, the German dictionary is the community frami build, the Danish one is Stavekontrolden 2.9.101, and English and Spanish match their upstream entry counts exactly. There is no dictionary upgrade available in any of the four — which moved the question from "get better data" to "use the data correctly", and that is where all four fixes above came from.

Is the bigger model worth it?

Mostly no, and it is better to say so.

Big Bad Betty costs roughly 30% more time and twice the memory, and the run scorecard puts the two models level. Split by language it gains three points of recall and three of precision — real, and not what twice the memory is usually expected to buy.

Where it does earn its size is narrow and specific: capitalisation (English 29% → 71%, German 79% → 93%, Spanish 80% → 100%), German commas (54% → 68%), and translation. It is level on spelling in every language. And it is worse on Danish — wrong-word recall halves, from 46% to 23% — which is the kind of result that makes a single headline number untrustworthy and is the reason this page splits everything by language.

This is the argument for spending effort on the deterministic layers rather than on recommending a bigger model: a dictionary fix or a disabled grammar rule helps both models equally, and helps the person running on an eight-gigabyte laptop most of all.

Reproducing this

Everything here comes from scripts/test-models.ts and scripts/test-translation.ts, run against the fixtures in sample_texts/. Results land in sample_texts/benchmark_results.json and its rendered .txt report, which includes every missed error and every false positive by name — that detail section is where all four of the fixes above were found.

The standing rule for changing a rule in this pipeline is the bar that found them: zero real errors found, at least one invented. Two of the four were caught by applying it to a rule nobody suspected. The ranked list of what is worth doing next lives in the repository as docs/language-quality-roadmap.md.
